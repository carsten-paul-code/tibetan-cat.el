* Slide 03 — Primer · What is a CAT-Tool? (≈2 min)

** Spoken

Before I go further, two short primers — for those who haven't worked with these categories of tool before, or who use them differently.

A CAT-Tool — and the C stands for *computer-assisted*, not *machine* — is a tool where the human translator stays in the chair. The point is not for the tool to translate the text. The point is for the tool to handle the bookkeeping around the translation, so the translator can spend their attention on the language.

Three classic pillars. [Point to the slide.]

[01] *Segmentation*. The tool chops the source into translatable units — a sentence, a clause, in our case a shad-bounded segment.

[02] *Translation memory*. The tool remembers every pair you've ever produced — source goes to target, target goes to source — and when something similar comes back, it offers you the previous translation as a suggestion.

[03] *Termbase*. A project-specific glossary, hand-curated, consulted at every token.

These are mature ideas. The existing tools — Wordfast, OmegaT, Trados, MemoQ — implement them very well, *for European-language commercial translation*. Tibetan, as we'll see, breaks all three pillars at once: the segmenter has nothing to bite on, the memory needs morphology rather than surface form, and the termbase has no place for Wylie or scholarly source attribution. So a Tibetan CAT tool is a different category — not a localised version of the generic one.

* Slide 04 — Primer · What is Emacs? (≈2 min)

** Spoken

The second primer is about Emacs.

Emacs is — depending on who you ask — a forty-nine-year-old text editor, or a Lisp environment, or an operating system with one working application. All three descriptions are accurate.

What makes it relevant for this talk is that Emacs is the environment where, for many academics, *most of their working day already happens*. [Point to the buffer mock.] Source texts in =.org= format, you can imagine it like markdown. Outlining and notes in org-mode. A Zettelkasten in org-roam. If org-mode is the document, Emacs is the desk it sits on.

The relevance for our story is this: when I decided to build a CAT-Tool, I was not asking myself "where shall I host this new application?" I was asking, "how do I add the missing tile to the desk that already has my Zettelkasten and my LaTeX pipeline on it?"

The answer was Emacs Lisp. Forty-nine years of editor evolution has produced a language and a runtime designed for exactly this: adding small, focused capabilities to a working environment without needing to rebuild it.

* Slide 07 — OCR & validation (≈2.5 min)

** Spoken

OCR for Tibetan is unreliable. Anyone who has tried this knows.
Stack splittings, missing tshegs, confused letters — ག for ད for
བ for པ. So the question wasn't whether I needed cleanup; the
question was *what kind of cleanup I trusted*.

I did not want a model that would rewrite whole lines based on
its guess at what was probably there. That's the kind of fix
that's invisibly wrong. What I wanted was a *checker* — something
that flagged the suspicious syllables and asked me before
changing anything.

So the validator is local — entirely in Emacs Lisp, no network
call. [Point to the validator panel.] It checks each syllable
against three sources: about seventeen-thousand vocabulary
entries — root and particle aware; about three thousand compound
terms; and a list of proper nouns, places, people, deities.

The flag has two levels. =:unknown= means the syllable is
structurally valid Tibetan but isn't in the dictionary —
something legitimate may have been missed. =:suspicious= means
the syllable violates Tibetan syllable structure — multiple
vowels, leading subscripts, that kind of thing. Real OCR errors.

Only what the local validator flags goes to Claude, and the
prompt is explicitly conservative: "only change syllables that
are clearly erroneous." [Indicate the reproducer.] Here's a real
example from the test suite — =sem-kyi= with the tsheg missing,
becomes =sem · kyi= with the tsheg restored, and Claude returns
the reason as "missing tsheg".

You can apply the corrections silently if you trust the run, or
ask per syllable. There's a defcustom for that.

[Indicate the screenshot slot.] What you'll see in the screenshot
once I've captured it for the final version — is the OCR review
session running on a real pecha scan. Highlighted syllables in
the buffer, suggestions in a side window.

And then — with clean text in hand, the harder problem was
inside the syllables themselves. Bialek's exercises were full of
cases where a particle is glued onto its stem.

[Advance to Slide 08.]

* Slide 08 — Particle recognition (≈3 min)

** Spoken

[Slide 08, indigo background.]

This is the slide where Bialek's textbook earns its keep —
because the textbook's exercises are exactly where this problem
is most clearly described.

The easy case for a particle is when it's its own syllable —
=gis=, =la=, =na=, you can see them with your eye. The hard case
is when a particle is *agglutinated* to its stem. One syllable
on the page, two morphemes in the grammar — and only context
tells you which.

[Point to the example.] =deys=. To the eye, this looks like a
single syllable. But it isn't. It's =de= — the demonstrative
"that" — plus the ergative particle =-s=. If you treat =deys= as
a single unit, every later layer collapses: clause segmentation,
argument structure, translation. Particle recognition is the
*preposition*; everything else builds on it.

The disambiguation needs grammar, not surface form. So the
system maintains a *whitelist* — drawn from Bialek's textbook —
of the eight pronouns and demonstratives that canonically take a
bare =-s= ergative in Classical Tibetan. [Point to the
whitelist.] =de=, ='di=, =kho=, =mo=, =nga=, =su=, =bu=, =mthu=.
Outside that whitelist, a final =-s= belongs to the syllable.
Inside it, the =-s= is the particle. There's a regression test
asserting this behaviour for every entry on the whitelist.

The reference is *Joanna Bialek's "A Textbook in Classical
Tibetan"* — and that reference travels deeper than the
whitelist. The relevant Bialek Portfolio sections, the same
sections a student would read for a particle exercise, are
inlined into Claude's system prompt every time the tool calls
Claude on a clause. So the model sees the same grammar reference
the human does. This isn't because Claude needs the help — it's
because the analysis should be *answerable* to the same source
the human translator is using.

And then — particles are one half of clause-level grammar. The
other half — the markers that *bind* clauses together — came in
the third semester, with deeper texts and the Bialek *Portfolio*.

[Advance to Slide 09.]

* Slide 09 — Converb recognition (≈2.5 min)

** Spoken

[Slide 09.]

Third semester. We started reading the Tigress jātaka. I was
also taking part in a Tibetan Buddhist texts reading group —
longer texts, more complex syntax than the textbook drills. And
that's where I hit the second half of clause-level grammar.

A converb isn't a standalone word. It's a suffix on a verb stem
that tells you *how* this clause connects to the next. Causally.
Conditionally. Simultaneously. Sequentially. Without converb
recognition, the segmenter has no clause boundaries to work
with — it can find a verb, but it doesn't know where the clause
that verb belongs to actually ends.

[Point to the suffix list on screen.] These are the workhorses,
straight from Bialek's *Portfolio*. =nas= — for sequential and
ablative — "having gone, then…". =te=, =ste=, =de= — coordinative
— a simple "and". =zhing=, =cing=, =shing= — simultaneous,
"while". =pas= and =bas= — causal, "because". =na= — conditional,
"if" or "when".

Each match attaches a *type* label to the clause: =causal=,
=conditional=, =simultaneous=, =sequential=. That label flows
into argument structure: a causal clause's subject often carries
the ergative; a conditional clause might lack a stated subject
entirely. The grammar bookkeeping isn't decoration — it's what
lets the segmenter make the right cuts.

And — same as with particles — the same *Portfolio* sections
that document each converb get inlined into Claude's system
prompt. The model sees the definition the human translator just
read.

And then — particles legible, converbs legible. Now the
segmenter has both halves, and can put a sentence together.

[Advance to Slide 10.]

* Slide 10 — Segmentation (≈2.5 min)

** Spoken

[Slide 10, saffron background.]

Tibetan does not punctuate the way European languages do.
*Tsheg* between syllables, *shad* to mark some clause ends,
sometimes a line break instead of a full stop, sometimes nothing
at all. For a generic CAT-Tool segmenter — built on full-stops
and sentence boundaries — this is fatal.

But with particles and converbs both legible, the segmenter has
something to work with. We call it *Round-2* — meaning the
second iteration, after the first proved that surface-pattern
splitting wasn't enough. It reads tsheg and shad, finds verb
endings, finds the converbs we just talked about, finds final
particles — and assigns argument structure using case-frames
from Hill 2010.

Here's the concrete example. Six words, =de la ma nyan pas
khros=. "Because (he) didn't listen to that, he got angry."
[Point.] The segmenter splits this into two clauses. Clause one
— =de la ma nyan pas= — is dependent, marked causal by the
converb =pas=. Clause two — =khros= — is the main clause, with
=khro= "to be angry" as its verb.

The system also chunks noun phrases and tags them with case:
ergative, genitive, dative-locative, terminative, ablative,
comitative.

Three keybindings expose this from any document. =C-c u B= runs
auto-analyze on the whole document. =C-c s N= creates one
sentence-level analysis file per sentence. =C-c s Z= re-segments
when the source has been edited.

This is the layer that produces the per-segment files I'll show
in a moment. Without segmentation that you can trust, none of
the rest of the tool would work.

And then — grammar was now legible end-to-end. But every
unfamiliar word still had to be looked up, in every dictionary,
every time.

[Advance to Slide 11.]

* Slide 11 — Multi-source dictionary (≈2 min)

** Spoken

Third semester. We started reading the vyāghrī-parivarta — the
Tigress jātaka — and I was also taking part in the Tibetan
Buddhist texts reading group. A new text every week, new
vocabulary every week. The bottleneck shifted from grammar to
*lookup*.

Tibetan philology depends on multiple dictionaries that disagree
productively. Steinert's aggregated SQLite — which inside it has
Hopkins, Valby, Waldo, Bialek, the 84000 glossary; Rangjung Yeshe
with its 162-thousand entries; Hopkins on its own as a bundled
local glossary — they all see the same word slightly differently,
and that difference is information.

Manually consulting four or five dictionaries per token took as
long as the translation itself. So the dictionary integration is
*every source, automatic, ranked, deduplicated*. [Point to the
list.]

The hand-curated *Resources* for the specific text being read
leads — that's bilingual German-English glosses I write myself
for the document I'm working on. Then the optional per-document
=#+TIBETAN_VOCAB_FILE= for one-off vocabulary. Then Steinert's
SQLite. Then Rangjung Yeshe. Then bundled Hopkins. And finally
DharmaMitra as an online AI fallback.

Deduplication is by gloss fingerprint — a normalised form of the
gloss itself — so when Hopkins and Rangjung Yeshe say roughly
the same thing in different words, the second one drops. When
they actually disagree, both stay.

Sanskrit only surfaces when an entry natively carries it. The
tool does not synthesise Sanskrit by reverse lookup. If
Steinert's Hopkins entry for =byang chub= carries =bodhi=, you
see it. If a Rangjung Yeshe entry doesn't carry Sanskrit, no
Sanskrit appears.

And then — I noticed something. I was redoing the same analysis
whenever I came back to a passage. The work needed to *persist*.

* Slide 12 — Per-segment Org file (≈2.5 min)

** Spoken

By the middle of the third semester I was reading several texts
in parallel — the Tigress, the texts in the reading group, my
own background reading — and I noticed I was redoing the same
analysis every time I came back to a passage. The work needed to
*persist*. This is the central artifact that came out of that.

If there is one slide where I want both Professor Kramer and Dr.
Nehrdich to look closely, it's this one.

[Point to the buffer mockup.] One file per shad-bounded clause —
=seg-014.org=, on screen. The file has two halves. The top half,
under =* Tibetan Analysis=, is *generated* and *regenerated*.
Wylie transliteration. Interlinear gloss. The Claude translation.
Grammar — including the Particle Map. Sentence structure. Verb
classification. The detailed multi-source dictionary entry.

The bottom half — =* My Notes=, =* Working Translation=, =*
Footnotes= — is *mine*. Written by hand. The translation I'm
actually producing. The footnote where I've decided how to
disambiguate a verse. The notes where I'm tracking unresolved
problems.

The contract is simple, and it's enforced in code. Re-running
the analyzer regenerates the =* Tibetan Analysis= tree — every
sub-section. It must *never* overwrite a sentence I wrote by
hand.

This contract is the thing that makes the system *trustable* for
real philological work. If I edit a footnote at midnight, and at
nine in the morning I batch-reanalyze two-hundred segments
because I've added a new dictionary entry, my footnote must
still be there. There is a regression test that asserts this for
every preserved section, on every reanalyze path. If the test
fails, the build fails. If the contract were ever to break, I
would catch it before shipping.

[Indicate the screenshot slot.] In the final version, the
screenshot here will be a real Emacs frame: source on the left,
the per-segment file on the right with all seven Tibetan-Analysis
subsections expanded.

And then — even with all this, draft translations still ate
hours. So I wired in two AI bridges. Very carefully.

* Slide 13 — AI bridges (≈3 min)

** Spoken

[Slide 12, indigo background. The two cards are side by side.]

Fourth semester. We started reading the Yogācārabhūmi —
Asaṅga's compendium of Yogācāra path doctrine. The text is
dense, technical, very long. Drafting an English translation of
every clause by hand — even with the dictionary right there —
was no longer realistic.

The temptation to use AI is obvious. The risk is also obvious:
an unaccountable, unattributable text, the kind that could end up
in a footnote masquerading as scholarship.

So both AI bridges are wired with care.

[Point to the left card.] The first is *DharmaMitra*. And here I
want to be explicit: DharmaMitra is Sebastian Nehrdich's project
— neural translation models trained for Sanskrit and Tibetan
domain text. The HTTP-and-server-sent-events client in this tool
talks to that infrastructure. It does not reproduce it. There
are two paths. A direct translation path: two parallel calls,
one for Tibetan, one for Sanskrit, both arriving as their own
top-level sections in the per-segment file. And a search-API
path, for parallel-text alignment: DharmaMitra returns candidate
Sanskrit parallels, Claude picks the best fit using a structured
=## Choice / ## Reason= schema, and the picked candidate lands
in the file with both the choice and the reasoning preserved.

[Point to the right card.] The second bridge is *Claude*, used
for general reasoning over the analysed segment. Claude is
queued — three concurrent at most, exponential backoff on rate
limits — and the system prompt is cached at ten percent of input
cost. Concretely: the dictionary entries, the Bialek Portfolio
sections, the verb classifier output — this whole context is
sent once, cached, and reused for every segment in a session.
Without that, long sessions would cost real money. With it, they
don't.

What both bridges share is *accountability*. Every output lands
in the per-segment file. Every output is replaceable — it's a
draft, not a verdict. And every output sits next to *my own*
working translation, which is the one that ends up in the paper.
The AI is in the workflow. It is not the workflow.

And then — for any of this to be scholarship-grade, the next
question is what Claude actually *sees*. That's the next slide.

[Advance to Slide 13.]

* Slide 14 — Claude context provision (≈2.5 min)

** Spoken

[Slide 13, indigo background.]

Before I show what came next, a moment on what Claude actually
*sees* when it's asked for a translation. Because an AI
suggestion is only as accountable as its inputs.

If Claude sees nothing — just the Tibetan source line — its
output is a guess. Plausible, often correct, but not
*answerable*. You can't argue with it on scholarly grounds; you
can only trust it or reject it.

So the system prompt for every Claude call bundles — [point to
slide] — the same scholarly references a human translator would
open. Bialek Portfolio sections, the relevant ones for the
particles in this segment. The multi-source dictionary entries
for every token. The Hill-2010 verb classification for every
verb. The Particle Map with case tags. In parallel-Sanskrit
mode, the Sanskrit text and its analysis. And the user's own
Working Translation, if I've been editing one.

[Brief pause.]

This bundle is large. Sending it for every segment would be
prohibitive. So the system prompt is wrapped in =gptel-cache= —
[point] — Anthropic's prompt caching. The full context is sent
once per session, then *cached at about ten percent of normal
input cost* for every subsequent call. Long sessions cost cents
instead of dollars.

The result, on the page, is what matters: a translation
suggestion that names the particles it relied on, the dictionary
sense it picked, the grammatical analysis it followed.
Replaceable. Argued with. Not a black box.

And then — the next layer of calibration: *which* language
Claude answers in.

[Slide 14.]

I'm writing my M.A. thesis in German.

For my M.A. thesis the target is German. For a future paper in

And then — beyond target language, the source side too has more
than one language at once.

* Slide 16 — Bilingual mode (≈3 min)

** Spoken

[Slide 13, maroon background.]

And the Yogācārabhūmi, of course, exists in Sanskrit *and* in
Tibetan. The texts that matter most for the kind of scholarship
Professor Kramer and Professor Schwerk supervise — abhidharma,
tantra, kāvya — almost always exist in both languages together.
Working from one without the other loses too much. The Tibetan
translation is itself a philological act; reading it without the
Sanskrit discards half the data.

So the parallel-Sanskrit mode is a proper top-level concept in
the tool. [Point to the source-mode header.] You toggle =#+SOURCE_MODE: parallel-sanskrit= on a document — there's a binding for
it, =C-c u z P= — and from that point, every per-segment file in
that document emits *six independent sections* per segment.

[Indicate the section list.] Three are Claude analyses. The
*Sanskrit Analysis* runs a Claude prompt configured for the
philological role of the Sanskrit. The *Tibetan Analysis* runs
the standard Tibetan-side prompt. And the *Combined Analysis* —
this is the one the audience may find most interesting — runs a
synthesis prompt that has read both prior outputs and produces a
combined translation, with an optional =** Sanskrit-Tibetan
Comparison= sub-section that surfaces points where the Tibetan
translator's choices were faithful, expansive, or interpretive.

Three more sections come from DharmaMitra. The Sanskrit and
Tibetan translations land as their own top-level sections — they
sit *outside* the Claude analyses, not inside, so they can be
referenced separately. The third DharmaMitra section is the
search-driven Sanskrit realignment from the previous slide:
*Sanskrit (DharmaMitra)*.

Six independent results, in one Org buffer, for one segment.
Sanskrit-first reading layout. All of them queued, all of them
respecting the prompt cache. User notes preserved on reanalyze.

[Indicate the screenshot slot.] Final version: a real
parallel-Sanskrit per-segment file in Emacs, all six sections
visible, both Devanagari and Uchen rendered.

And then — the question is no longer "what does this feature

* Slide 17 — The scholar's workflow (≈1.5 min)

** Spoken

This is the loop. [Point to the diagram.]

You start with a source — Tibetan or Sanskrit, in =.org= format.
=C-c u P= prepares the document — applies the segmenter,
inserts the proper heading structure. =C-c u B= runs
auto-analyze on the prepared document, creating one
=seg-NNN.org= file per segment, and immediately fires the
async-queued Claude and DharmaMitra calls.

While those are coming back, you read. Uchen, Wylie, the
interlinear gloss, the grammar with the Particle Map, the
detailed dictionary. By the time you've read the segment, the
draft translation has usually arrived in the file already.

You refine. You write your working translation. You add notes,
add footnotes. None of this is overwritten when you reanalyze.

You combine — =C-c u C= stitches all the per-segment files into
a single combined document with all your working translations
threaded together. And you export. =Org= to LuaLaTeX to PDF, the
same pipeline I use for every paper.

Eight stages, one editor. The translator never has to leave the
buffer. Everything that you write by hand survives every
regeneration. That's the design.
